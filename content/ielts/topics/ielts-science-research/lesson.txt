[[LANGUAGE_BLOCK ielts-science-research-scientific-evidence]]
expression: scientific evidence
meaning_vi: bằng chứng khoa học
pronunciation: /ˌsaɪənˈtɪfɪk ˈevɪdəns/
collocations: evaluate scientific evidence
grammar_pattern: Noun phrase: use as a subject, object, or complement after a suitable preposition.
example: Our team will distinguish scientific evidence from an attractive story when reporting a fictional survey of neighbourhood gardens.
common_error: Evidence is uncountable; avoid evidences in this general use.
[[/LANGUAGE_BLOCK]]

[[LANGUAGE_BLOCK ielts-science-research-research-methods]]
expression: research methods
meaning_vi: phương pháp nghiên cứu
pronunciation: /rɪˈsɜːtʃ ˈmeθədz/
collocations: compare research methods
grammar_pattern: Noun phrase: use as a subject, object, or complement after a suitable preposition.
example: The research methods document compares direct observation with residents' self-reported estimates.
common_error: Methods are procedures, not the findings themselves.
[[/LANGUAGE_BLOCK]]

[[LANGUAGE_BLOCK ielts-science-research-sample-size]]
expression: sample size
meaning_vi: cỡ mẫu
pronunciation: /ˈsɑːmpəl saɪz/
collocations: increase the sample size
grammar_pattern: Noun phrase: use as a subject, object, or complement after a suitable preposition.
example: The sample size is forty gardens, and the report will explain why that number limits detailed subgroup comparisons.
common_error: Size refers to the number of observations in the sample.
[[/LANGUAGE_BLOCK]]

[[LANGUAGE_BLOCK ielts-science-research-control-group]]
expression: control group
meaning_vi: nhóm đối chứng
pronunciation: /kənˈtrəʊl ɡruːp/
collocations: include a control group
grammar_pattern: Noun phrase: use as a subject, object, or complement after a suitable preposition.
example: A control group will follow the usual gardening routine while another group tries a different watering schedule.
common_error: Use a before one control group.
[[/LANGUAGE_BLOCK]]

[[LANGUAGE_BLOCK ielts-science-research-reliable-results]]
expression: reliable results
meaning_vi: kết quả đáng tin cậy
pronunciation: /rɪˈlaɪəbəl rɪˈzʌlts/
collocations: obtain reliable results
grammar_pattern: Noun phrase: use as a subject, object, or complement after a suitable preposition.
example: To obtain reliable results, observers will use the same recording sheet and compare how they classify uncertain cases.
common_error: Reliable describes consistency; it does not automatically prove a causal claim.
[[/LANGUAGE_BLOCK]]

[[LANGUAGE_BLOCK ielts-science-research-ethical-approval]]
expression: ethical approval
meaning_vi: sự phê duyệt về đạo đức nghiên cứu
pronunciation: /ˈeθɪkəl əˈpruːvəl/
collocations: obtain ethical approval
grammar_pattern: Noun phrase: use as a subject, object, or complement after a suitable preposition.
example: The team will seek ethical approval before collecting any identifiable interviews and will explain withdrawal rights.
common_error: Approval is uncountable in this administrative sense.
[[/LANGUAGE_BLOCK]]

[[LANGUAGE_BLOCK ielts-science-research-data-collection]]
expression: data collection
meaning_vi: việc thu thập dữ liệu
pronunciation: /ˈdeɪtə kəˈlekʃən/
collocations: systematic data collection
grammar_pattern: Noun phrase: use as a subject, object, or complement after a suitable preposition.
example: The data collection schedule covers weekdays and weekends to avoid observing only one type of garden use.
common_error: Collection is the noun; collect is the verb.
[[/LANGUAGE_BLOCK]]

[[LANGUAGE_BLOCK ielts-science-research-peer-review]]
expression: peer review
meaning_vi: sự phản biện của chuyên gia cùng lĩnh vực
pronunciation: /pɪə rɪˈvjuː/
collocations: independent peer review
grammar_pattern: Noun phrase: use as a subject, object, or complement after a suitable preposition.
example: Independent peer review will assess whether the conclusions follow from the methods and observations.
common_error: Peer review is a process, not a guarantee of perfect accuracy.
[[/LANGUAGE_BLOCK]]

[[LANGUAGE_BLOCK ielts-science-research-causal-relationship]]
expression: causal relationship
meaning_vi: mối quan hệ nhân quả
pronunciation: /ˈkɔːzəl rɪˈleɪʃənʃɪp/
collocations: establish a causal relationship
grammar_pattern: Noun phrase: use as a subject, object, or complement after a suitable preposition.
example: The report will not claim a causal relationship between garden visits and wellbeing from an observational survey alone.
common_error: Do not confuse causal with casual.
[[/LANGUAGE_BLOCK]]

[[LANGUAGE_BLOCK ielts-science-research-statistical-analysis]]
expression: statistical analysis
meaning_vi: phân tích thống kê
pronunciation: /stəˈtɪstɪkəl əˈnæləsɪs/
collocations: conduct statistical analysis
grammar_pattern: Noun phrase: use as a subject, object, or complement after a suitable preposition.
example: The statistical analysis plan will be written before the team inspects the final results.
common_error: Analysis is singular; analyses is plural.
[[/LANGUAGE_BLOCK]]

[[LANGUAGE_BLOCK ielts-science-research-research-limitations]]
expression: research limitations
meaning_vi: hạn chế của nghiên cứu
pronunciation: /rɪˈsɜːtʃ ˌlɪmɪˈteɪʃənz/
collocations: acknowledge research limitations
grammar_pattern: Noun phrase: use as a subject, object, or complement after a suitable preposition.
example: The research limitations section will describe missing observations and the lack of winter measurements.
common_error: Limitations are constraints on interpretation, not excuses to omit results.
[[/LANGUAGE_BLOCK]]

[[LANGUAGE_BLOCK ielts-science-research-practical-applications]]
expression: practical applications
meaning_vi: ứng dụng thực tiễn
pronunciation: /ˈpræktɪkəl ˌæplɪˈkeɪʃənz/
collocations: explore practical applications
grammar_pattern: Noun phrase: use as a subject, object, or complement after a suitable preposition.
example: Possible practical applications include improving the observation process before deciding whether to fund a larger study.
common_error: Applications can mean uses, not only software or forms.
[[/LANGUAGE_BLOCK]]

[CONTENT ielts-science-research-proposal]
type: email
text:
<<<
Dear planning group,
We are a fictional research team designing a neighbourhood survey. Please compare the priorities below before our next meeting.
Our team will distinguish scientific evidence from an attractive story when reporting a fictional survey of neighbourhood gardens.
The research methods document compares direct observation with residents' self-reported estimates.
The sample size is forty gardens, and the report will explain why that number limits detailed subgroup comparisons.
A control group will follow the usual gardening routine while another group tries a different watering schedule.
To obtain reliable results, observers will use the same recording sheet and compare how they classify uncertain cases.
The team will seek ethical approval before collecting any identifiable interviews and will explain withdrawal rights.
The data collection schedule covers weekdays and weekends to avoid observing only one type of garden use.
Independent peer review will assess whether the conclusions follow from the methods and observations.
The report will not claim a causal relationship between garden visits and wellbeing from an observational survey alone.
The statistical analysis plan will be written before the team inspects the final results.
The research limitations section will describe missing observations and the lack of winter measurements.
Possible practical applications include improving the observation process before deciding whether to fund a larger study.
Please recommend one change that we can explain and evaluate, while acknowledging what this proposal does not yet establish.
Regards, the planning coordinator
>>>
[/CONTENT]

[CONTENT ielts-science-research-discussion]
type: dialogue
text:
<<<
An (nam): Let us review this proposal from the perspective of people who will actually use it.
Lan (nữ): I agree. We should compare the intended benefits with practical barriers and decide what to ask the planning group.
An: Scientific evidence would be stronger if readers could see how observations were collected rather than only a confident headline.
Lan: Research methods should fit the question; a survey of opinions cannot directly measure the number of insects in a garden.
An: A larger sample size may help, but adding only gardens from the same street could leave other areas unrepresented.
Lan: A control group would let us compare the new routine with what would happen without the change.
An: Reliable results depend on consistent observations, so we should practise recording a few examples before starting.
Lan: Ethical approval should come before recruitment, and participants still need a clear explanation in ordinary language.
An: Data collection only on sunny afternoons could give a misleading picture of how often people use the space.
Lan: Peer review can identify weaknesses, but readers should still examine the evidence instead of assuming a reviewed claim is final.
An: A causal relationship cannot be assumed just because the most frequent visitors also report feeling happier.
Lan: Statistical analysis should answer our original questions rather than search until one comparison looks impressive.
An: Research limitations should be visible in the summary too, because many readers will not reach the final page.
Lan: Practical applications may begin with a better pilot study rather than an immediate recommendation for every neighbourhood.
An: We have several reasonable priorities, but the budget cannot cover every suggestion immediately.
Lan: Let us recommend one change, explain our reasons, and identify evidence that would tell us whether it worked.
>>>
[/CONTENT]
